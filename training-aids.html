Golf Training Aids: What Works and How to Use Them

A good golf training aid gives you instant feedback that your eyes and body cannot give you on their own. A bad one sits in the garage. This guide covers the training aids our coaches actually use, what problem each one solves, and how to get real value from it.

The one rule: Buy a training aid to fix a problem you already know you have. If you cannot name the problem in one sentence, a lesson will help more than any gadget.

Our eight favorite golf training aids

Alignment sticks

Fixes: aiming right or left without knowing it.

Lay one stick along your target line and one along your toe line. Most golfers are surprised to learn they aim several yards off. Sticks also work as a gate for your swing path or a guide for ball position.

Best for: every golfer. If you buy only one aid, buy these.

Impact bag

Fixes: flipping the wrists and scooping at the ball.

Swing slowly into the bag and freeze at impact. Your hands should be ahead of the clubhead, your weight on your front foot, and your chest turning toward the target. Ten slow reps teach the feel faster than a hundred range balls.

Best for: golfers who hit thin or fat iron shots.

Putting mirror

Fixes: eyes and shoulders that are out of position at address.

The mirror shows whether your eyes sit over the ball and whether your shoulders are square. Use it for five minutes before every putting session to check your setup, then put it away and putt to real holes.

Best for: anyone who misses short putts in the same direction.

Weighted swing trainer

Fixes: a rushed, jerky transition from backswing to downswing.

The extra weight makes it hard to snatch the club down from the top. Make slow, full swings and let the weight pull your arms through. It also makes an excellent warm-up before a round.

Best for: golfers whose tempo falls apart under pressure.

Home practice net

Fixes: not having time to get to the range.

A net in a garage or backyard lets you make real swings in short sessions. Pair it with a hitting mat and an alignment stick so every swing still has a target and a check point.

Best for: busy golfers who can practice 15 minutes at a time.

Personal launch monitor

Fixes: guessing how far each club really goes.

Knowing your true carry distance with every club is one of the fastest ways to lower scores. Hit ten shots with each club, ignore the best and worst two, and write down the average of the rest.

Best for: intermediate golfers ready to sharpen distance control.

Putting gate

Fixes: an open or closed putter face at impact.

A gate is just two pegs set slightly wider than your putter head, a few inches in front of the ball. If the ball rolls cleanly through, your face was square. You can build one from two tees for free.

Best for: golfers who start putts off line.

A plain towel

Fixes: poor chipping contact and arms that separate from the body.

Place a towel a few inches behind the ball and chip without touching it to train clean contact. Tuck it under both armpits during half swings to keep your arms and body moving together.

Best for: every golfer, and it costs nothing.

How to get your money's worth

  • Use one aid at a time. Working on three fixes at once usually means fixing none of them.
  • Alternate with and without. Hit five balls with the aid, then five without it. The goal is to keep the feel when the aid is gone.
  • Go slow first. Make half-speed swings until the new move feels natural, then build back up to full speed.
  • Set an end date. Most training aids do their job in three to six weeks. If nothing has changed by then, ask a coach whether you are working on the right problem.
From Coach Marisol: "The best training aid in the world is a clear target. Put a stick down, pick a flag, and commit to every shot. The gadgets help, but they only work if you know where you are trying to go."
